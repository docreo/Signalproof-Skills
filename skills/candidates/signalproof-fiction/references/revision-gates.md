# Literary Revision Gates

## First rule: utility over provenance

The question is not whether a machine proposed a word. The question is whether that word serves the work.

### Preserve authorial state

- Compare every revised paragraph against the original; restore original wording when a small requested fix triggered unnecessary rewriting.
- Confirm each claim, implication, design/magic rule, scene objective, character motive, and historical detail retains its intended strength and meaning.
- Do not add hedges or disclaimers the author did not request. Do not erase purposeful uncertainty, either.
- Do not add new sentences, citations, explanations, or examples solely to make a passage sound comprehensive.
- Check every previously accepted author correction survives. Use the project's own revision-history checklist; never fabricate missing historic revision items.
- Remove em and en dashes when forbidden by the project's style policy.
- Perform an aloud-read or rhythm check; prose should sound like distinct people, not generic committee minutes.
- Log restorations and material edits in an AI/editorial use record.

### Story addition test

For each proposed addition ask:
1. Does it *change what happens*, sharpen a physical event, expose subtext, distinguish a character, or increase felt stakes?
2. Would a reader understand the scene without this new explanation?
3. Is this information already present elsewhere?
4. Does it revise a canonical rule, chronology, emotional decision, relationship, or reveal?
5. Does its sentence structure reflect the actual narrator and speaking character?

Reject or flag expansions that only explain what is already evident. Preserve or add length when the scene truly needs time, specificity, or consequence.

### Story cut test

For each proposed cut ask:
1. Will we lose an essential physical orientation, cause, rule, setup, or payoff?
2. Is this information better shown by an existing action or dialogue exchange?
3. Will the cut make a character inexplicably act or know something?
4. Is the sentence deliberately rhythmic, emotional, or thematically resonant rather than generic summary?

Cut repetition, not world logic. A repeated principle may be necessary when it changes the outcome of a new scene.

### High-frequency red flags

- The same authority/mechanics rule restated without new stakes.
- Multiple scenes whose main result is the same discovery or the same caution.
- Identical rhetorical shapes in every character's speech.
- Closing sentences that merely restate the preceding paragraph.
- "Not X, but Y" and stacked negation as habitual narration.
- Excess use of "pretend," "perhaps," "may," "cannot yet," "not established" as filler rather than intentional characterization.
- Explanations of why a scene matters immediately after showing the reader why.
- Characters sounding equally formal during danger, intimacy, mourning, and command.
- Magical solutions that cancel previously costly choices.
- Unsourced character names or retcons introduced in a remaster without marking them proposed.

### Whole-chapter structural review

Before micro-edits, document the scene chain: goal, obstacle, choice, consequence, transition. Examine whether investigation, training, travel, or exposition beats repeat without escalation. In slow sequences, add friction or consolidate scenes if needed; do not indiscriminately delete all thoughtful passages. In action, honor tactical geography and material limits.

### Chapter-length policy

No default minimum, maximum, or percentage cut. A 4,500-word chapter may become 6,200 or 3,800 words. Assess whether each sequence earns its space. Length changes are outcomes, not goals.

### Acceptance threshold

A completed remaster requires a changed and checked manuscript, a complete diff, verified continuity, and explicit author review status. A status label or a small set of cuts is not a whole-book revision.
